This blog is called <span class="chulapa">Chulapa</span> because of the letters
in its name. When I was building the Jekyll theme, I borrowed the name from a
Madrid typeface I wanted to use on the site. The blog took the theme’s name, and
the word you see in the headline is still set in that typeface. To see where it
came from, look up at a street corner in the old city.

## From painted tiles to type

In La Latina, the Cava Baja street sign gives the name of the street in painted
capitals. It belongs to a set of ceramic plaques that Madrid commissioned from
[Alfredo Ruiz de
Luna](https://cultura.castillalamancha.es/museos/exposiciones-temporales/alfredo-ruiz-de-luna-un-ceramista-talaverano-en-madrid)
in 1992. A municipal record puts the project at around 1,500 plaques for some
400 streets. [Pedro Reina’s photograph of the Cava Baja
sign](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Calle_de_la_Cava_Baja_(Madrid)1.jpg)
· [Madrid’s municipal
record](https://www.madrid.es/UnidadWeb/Contenidos/Publicaciones/JuntaDistritos/ActasCentro/2015/Acta17diciembre15.pdf)

The painted letters also caught the eye of designer [Silvia Fernández
Palomar](https://www.azkunazentroa.eus/es/artistas/silvia-fernandez-palomar/),
who developed Ferpal Sans. Her lettering appeared alongside [Mercedes
deBellard](https://www.domestika.org/en/mercedes_debellard)’s illustrations in
the 2018 San Isidro campaign. [Joan Carles
Casasín](https://tipografies.com/about) and [Pablo
Gámez](https://typemedia2017.com/pablo/) developed Chulapa from that original
idea. Madrid made the typeface freely available on May 15, 2019, for San Isidro.
Its letters retain some of the irregularity of the painted signs, and alternate
forms and ligatures give neighboring letters different ways to sit together.
[2018 campaign
artwork](https://www.behance.net/gallery/71588939/Campana-San-Isidro-2018-Madrid)
· [Madrid’s typeface specimen and
download](https://identidad.madrid.es/la-chulapa/)

You can still see it around the city. Madrid’s 2026 identity guide uses an
adaptation of Chulapa Bold for the city wordmark. In May that year, the
municipal transport company, EMT Madrid, printed the typeface and red carnations
on T-shirts and tote bags for San Isidro. [Madrid’s typography
guide](https://identidad.madrid.es/tipografias/) · [EMT’s San Isidro
collection](https://diario.madrid.es/blog/notas-de-prensa/emt-madrid-lanza-una-edicion-especial-de-camisetas-y-bolsas-de-tela-para-festejar-san-isidro/)

## The chulapa behind the name

A *chulapa*, like her male counterpart the *chulapo*, is a figure from the
popular culture of Madrid’s working-class neighborhoods. The festival dress
typically includes a polka-dot dress or skirt, a white headscarf with a
carnation and a fringed *mantón de Manila*, an embroidered shawl. The outfit is
still worn at local celebrations. For San Isidro in 2025, the city even made
sewing patterns available for people to make their own. [Madrid’s folklore
guide](https://www.esmadrid.com/folclore-madrid) · [The city’s dressmaking
project](https://diario.madrid.es/blog/notas-de-prensa/el-ayuntamiento-invita-a-disenar-y-confeccionar-trajes-de-chulapo-y-chulapa-para-celebrar-san-isidro-2025/)

On May 15, Madrid celebrates its patron saint, San Isidro Labrador. At the
Pradera de San Isidro, the meadow that still hosts the festivities, people
gather for the *romería* and eat *rosquillas*, the festival’s ring-shaped
pastries. The crowds in Francisco Goñi’s 1907 photograph had another attraction
to try that year, a newly installed carousel. [Madrid’s San Isidro
route](https://www.esmadrid.com/ruta-san-isidro)

In August the fiestas move to the streets of La Latina for *La Paloma*, whose
main day is August 15. Alongside the celebrations for the Virgen de la Paloma,
there’s a *verbena*, an open-air neighborhood party with music and dancing.
Christian Franzen’s photograph shows how the old chapel looked during the summer
of 1895. [Madrid’s guide to the August
fiestas](https://www.esmadrid.com/en/whats-on/august-fiestas-madrid)

## A dance on one tile

The *chotis* is danced in pairs, traditionally to the sound of an *organillo*,
or barrel organ. One dancer turns in place while the other circles them. The old
test is whether you can do it without stepping off a single floor tile. One of
the best-known tunes is *Madrid*, often called *Madrid, Madrid, Madrid*, by the
Mexican composer [Agustín
Lara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agust%C3%ADn_Lara). The
recording below is by [Lola
Flores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lola_Flores).
[Madrid’s guide to the
chotis](https://www.esmadrid.com/sites/default/files/esmadrid_magazine_mayo_2024.pdf)
· [Gran Vía’s musical
history](https://www.esmadrid.com/file/189655/download?token=GQKiDHDY)
